# How it's made — the process is the shape

*Opened 2026-09-16, after four attempts at a slice of [a dish]. The fourth was accurate about the
object and still wrong, and the owner said why in one sentence:*

> *"think about how it is made too right? so the roll should make sense in terms of how it is rolled
> up and the sliced into round slices — thats why rarely are they displayed like you did or if it
> close it is in a pile and they have more congruence"*

**He was right, and the error was bigger than every colour and proportion I had been fixing.** I had
drawn six slices of [a dish] as six independent objects, each with its own randomly arranged filling.
But all six came off **one roll**. The fillings were laid in a line on a flat sheet of nori, rolled
once, and then cut — so the cross-section is *the same all the way down the cylinder*. Six slices of
one roll are **siblings**, not strangers. Mine were six different rolls with one slice each, and no
amount of correcting the bundle was ever going to fix that.

---

## The rule, and it is one sentence

> **Variation enters at the step it actually entered, and nowhere earlier.**

Anything decided before the copies were separated is **shared by all of them**. Anything decided
after is what differs. Get this backwards and the result reads as *generated* — and nobody will be
able to tell you why, because every individual object is fine.

In the [a dish]:

| step | when it happened | so across six slices it is |
|---|---|---|
| which fillings, and in what order | **once**, on the flat sheet | **identical** |
| how tightly it was rolled | **once** | **identical** |
| where the knife fell | per slice | **different** — this is the only honest source of size variation |
| how it landed on the plate | per slice | **different** — rotation, lean, overlap |

So: one filling arrangement, six times, at six rotations, at six thicknesses. That is the picture.
Six arrangements is a picture of something that cannot exist.

## Two more, learned the same afternoon and both one level DOWN

**A made thing is made of made things. Recurse.**
Getting the roll right is not getting the slice right. Every piece inside it was also cut by
somebody, and **the section of each piece is decided by how THAT piece was cut** — they were not all
cut the same way, so they cannot all be the same shape:

| piece | how it was cut | so its section is |
|---|---|---|
| pickled radish | a baton sawn from something big | a rounded rectangle — **the only honest bar in there** |
| omelette | cooked as a SHEET, then sliced | wide and thin, with the fold showing as a paler line |
| carrot | **julienned** | four or five thin slivers with gaps — *never one fat orange block* |
| spinach | blanched and **wrung out** | a dense band with no straight edge anywhere |

The owner's words, and they are the test: *"a carrot isnt square like that — at best square-ish."*
**When every part of an object is the same primitive in a different colour, the recursion has not
been done.** That is the second most common way a drawing reads as generated.

**Commit to a KIND. Do not average a family.**
*"…but depends on the type of [a dish]."* A tuna roll, a cheese roll and a vegetable roll have
different things inside and they are different shapes. Averaging the family gives you a filling that
no actual roll contains. Pick one and be right about that one.

**And the cheapest check of all: what does the thing's own paperwork say is in it?** The page the
drawing sits on listed rice, gim, egg, spinach and sesame oil. The drawing had burdock, beef, crab
stick and cucumber — none of them on the list, three inches away. **An ingredient list, a bill of
materials, a parts diagram: that is the process input, written down, and it is usually already in
the file you are working in.**

**One warning from doing it:** getting each section's shape right made every piece smaller, and the
filling went straight back to being a badge in a field of rice — the fault from two passes earlier.
**Fixing shape is not permission to lose mass.** Four ingredients fill a roll as completely as eight,
because each one is bigger. A handful of spinach is a handful, not a garnish.

## And two more, which are about the CONTAINER

**A cut piece of a long thing is a PUCK, not a coin.**
A [a dish] slice is about 2 cm thick on a 4 cm face. Lying face-up with the camera above it you see the
cut face **and a band of the side** — and when several are dominoed together you see the side of each
one in front of the face of the next. Four versions drew flat discs. A disc never sits on the board;
it lies on it like a sticker. **Whenever you cut a length off something, ask how thick the cut was,
and draw that.**

**A container ROUNDS what it holds.**
The owner: *"its more of a pile that gets rounded together inside a cilinder."* The filling was built
as a rectangular grid of long rows, which is what a pile looks like **before** it is wrapped. Rolling
squeezes it into a roughly circular cluster, so each row is a **chord of a circle** — narrow at the
top and bottom, widest across the middle — and the whole pile is smaller and tighter than a grid
wants to be. The widths are computed (`2·C·√(1−t²)`), not chosen.

The general form: **a soft thing takes the shape of what encloses it.** Dough in a tin, filling in a
roll, wool in a sack, cargo in a hull, grain in a silo. If your contents have square corners and the
container is round, the drawing is of the moment before it was closed.

## When they are together, they are lit together

Shape is not the end of it. Several correct objects in one frame will still read as **stickers laid
on top of one another** until they are lit as one scene:

- **They cast on each other.** Not just onto the ground — onto the neighbour they lean against. This
  is the single change that turns an overlap into a stack.
- **Each has a terminator.** A face is a plane, and the far side of a plane is darker. One exposure
  edge to edge is the flat look, whatever the shading inside the object.
- **Every inside corner is dark.** Where a face meets a wall, where an object meets the ground,
  light does not reach. It is the cheapest depth in the box.

**And in 2D, the order you draw in is part of the lighting model.** A cast shadow can only land on
something already painted. With a key from the upper left, shadows fall to the lower right, so the
objects on the right must be drawn FIRST. Painted the other way round, every shadow lands on
background that the next object covers up — and the result looks precisely like no shadows at all,
with nothing in the code to show for it.

## The five questions, before you draw anything

**1 · How was it made? Say the steps out loud.**
Not "it is a bowl" — *thrown on a wheel, trimmed, glazed, fired*. The steps are the answer sheet for
everything below.

**2 · What does each step leave behind?**
Every process leaves a signature, and the signature is most of what makes a drawing convincing:

| process | what it leaves |
|---|---|
| **rolled** | a spiral, a seam where the sheet ends, a cross-section that repeats along the whole length |
| **cut / sliced** | a flat face at an angle you chose, tool marks across it, and **a face that is the same on every slice from the same piece** |
| **thrown on a wheel** | concentric rings inside, a foot ring, a slight wobble off true |
| **cast or moulded** | a mould seam, a sprue mark, identical siblings and *no* honest variation at all |
| **extruded** | a constant cross-section, a cut at each end |
| **woven** | a warp you see and a weft you half see, and a selvedge at the edges |
| **stacked or piled** | the ones underneath are squashed; the top one is not |
| **poured** | a meniscus, a level surface finding gravity, a rim where it stopped |
| **grown** | asymmetry that follows a direction — light, gravity, a stem — never random asymmetry |
| **worn / used** | the damage is where the hands go, and nowhere else |

**3 · What do the copies share?**
From one batch, one roll, one mould, one sheet: what is common? Draw the common thing ONCE and
reuse it. This is where most "generated" art fails.

**4 · How did it come to rest?**
Objects do not float in a row at equal spacing. They **lean, shingle, stack, sag, roll, touch, and
settle against each other**. A cut cylinder standing on its face topples into its neighbour. If your
arrangement is evenly spaced and upright, ask what is holding it there — and if the answer is
"nothing", that is a drawing of a diagram.

**5 · What did the tool leave?**
Knife marks, brush direction, finger dents in clay, the drip on the glaze, the flat spot where it sat
while it cooled. One of these, placed where it belongs, is worth more than an hour of colour work.

## The tell, in reverse

When a picture of several things reads as generated and nobody can say why, check in this order:

1. **Are the copies siblings or strangers?** Strangers that should be siblings is the commonest fault
   and the hardest to see, because every individual object is correct.
2. **Is anything randomised that was decided before the copies separated?**
3. **Are they arranged, or are they just placed?** Evenly spaced, non-touching, all upright is the
   signature of a loop that ran `n` times.
4. **Does any surface show the tool?**

## And the smaller lesson that came with it

Three attempts before this one were spent correcting the object — the nori's thickness, the size of
the bundle, the exact yellow of the danmuji — and every correction was right and none of them was the
problem. **Reasoning from the NAME of a thing produces a diagram of the name.** Reasoning from how it
is made produces the thing.

If you cannot reach a reference, say so and ask for one. That should happen on the second failed
attempt, not the fourth.
